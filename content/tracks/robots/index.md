For middle schoolers, nothing beats code that makes something roll across the floor. I taught this in person with a small Scribbler robot from Georgia Tech's robotics education program, programmed in Python. The robot has wheels, a speaker, light sensors, infrared obstacle sensors and a camera, which turns out to be just enough to teach most of programming *and* a surprising amount of physics.

| # | Unit | What it covers | Grades | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hello, robot | connecting, `beep`, `speak`, making it say your name | 5–8 | Planned |
| 2 | Driving | `forward`, `turn`, timing, drawing a square; why it never comes out quite square | 5–8 | Planned |
| 3 | Loops that dance | repeat, sequences of moves, a robot dance set to beeps | 5–8 | Planned |
| 4 | Sensing the world | light sensors and infrared obstacle sensors; follow a light, stop before a wall | 6–8 | Planned |
| 5 | The robot's eye | taking pictures, pixels as numbers, finding a colour | 6–8 | Planned |
| 6 | Robots that decide | if / else with sensors, a simple tic-tac-toe player, a maze runner | 6–8 | Planned |

**No robot? No problem.** Each unit will come with an on-screen robot that runs the same programs, so the ideas work at home too.

The sensors connect straight to [The invisible world](../invisible-world/index.md): the obstacle sensor works by shining infrared light you can't see and checking whether it bounces back.
