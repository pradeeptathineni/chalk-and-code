## Start here

Hold a fridge magnet a finger's width from the fridge door. Let go. It jumps the gap. Nothing touched it. So what pushed it?

## The common wrong idea

"To push or pull something, you have to touch it."

Most pushes and pulls you feel *are* touching: kicking a ball, pushing a door. But three of the most important forces in the universe work across empty space: **gravity**, **magnetism** and **electric force** (the force behind static electricity).

## The idea

Scientists explain these forces with the idea of a **field**. A magnet fills the space around it with an invisible magnetic field. Anything magnetic that sits in that field feels a push or a pull. You can't see the field, but you can map it: a compass needle turns to line up with it, wherever you put it.

| Force | What makes the field | What feels it | Pulls, pushes, or both? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gravity | anything with mass | anything with mass | only pulls |
| Magnetism | magnets, and electric currents | magnets, iron, steel, nickel | both |
| Electric force | electric charges | electric charges | both |

All three fields get **weaker with distance**, and fast. Move a magnet twice as far away and its pull drops to well under half. That's why the fridge magnet only jumps when it's close.

## Worked example

Why doesn't the Earth's gravity pull you into the ground hard enough to hurt, while it pulls the Moon all the way around us?

1. Gravity only pulls, and it pulls on every bit of mass.
2. You have a small mass, so the pull on you (your weight) is modest, and the ground pushes back to hold you up.
3. The Moon is enormous, so even though it's far away and the field is weaker there, the total pull is huge. With nothing to hold it up, the Moon keeps falling *around* the Earth instead of into it.

## Your turn (faded example)

A paperclip sits 1 cm from a magnet and jumps to it. Move it to 10 cm and nothing happens.

1. Does the magnet's field still reach 10 cm? ___
2. What happens to a field's strength as you move away? ___
3. So why didn't the paperclip move? ___

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The field does still reach 10 cm, but it's much weaker there. The pull is too small to beat the friction holding the paperclip to the table, so it stays put.

</details>

## Practice

1. Which of the three forces can only pull, never push?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Gravity. Magnets and charges can push or pull, depending on which ends or signs face each other.

</details>

2. How could you find out which way a magnetic field points at a spot on your desk?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Put a compass there. Its needle lines up with the field. Move the compass around to map the whole field.

</details>
