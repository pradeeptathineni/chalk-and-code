> **After this lesson you can:**
> - explain what electric charge is and where it comes from
> - predict whether two charged things attract or repel
> - explain why a rubbed balloon sticks to a wall, and what a spark is

## Start here

Rub a balloon on your hair, then hold it near a wall. It sticks. Hold it near a thin stream of water from a tap and the water bends towards it. What changed about the balloon when you rubbed it?

## The common wrong idea

"Rubbing *makes* electricity."

Rubbing doesn't create anything. Everything is already full of electric charges: **protons** with a positive charge and **electrons** with a negative charge, in equal numbers, so they cancel out. Rubbing just **moves some electrons** from one thing to another. Now one thing has extra electrons (negative) and the other is missing some (positive).

## The idea

- There are two kinds of charge: **positive (+)** and **negative (−)**.
- **Like charges repel. Unlike charges attract.** (Sound familiar? Magnets follow the same rule.)
- Rub a balloon on your hair and electrons move from your hair to the balloon. The balloon is now negative and your hair is positive. That's why your hair stands up towards the balloon, and why each hair pushes away from the others.
- A charged balloon can even attract a wall that has no charge at all. Its negative charge pushes the electrons in the wall's surface slightly away, leaving the surface a little positive, and that pulls the balloon in.
- When enough charge builds up, it can jump through the air all at once: a **spark**. Touching a doorknob after shuffling across a carpet gives a tiny spark. A thundercloud builds up so much charge that the spark is a mile long: **lightning**.

## Worked example

You rub a balloon on a wool sweater, and electrons move from the wool to the balloon. Then you hold the balloon near the sweater. What happens?

1. The balloon gained electrons, so it's **negative**.
2. The sweater lost electrons, so it's **positive**.
3. Unlike charges attract, so the balloon is **pulled back towards the sweater**.

## Your turn (faded example)

You rub two balloons on your hair and hang them side by side on strings.

1. Both balloons gained electrons, so both are ___.
2. Like or unlike charges? ___
3. So the balloons ___.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are negative. Like charges repel, so the balloons swing apart from each other.

</details>

## Practice

1. Why do you get more static shocks on dry winter days than on humid summer days?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Water in the air lets extra charge leak away slowly and quietly. In dry air the charge has nowhere to go, so it builds up on you until it jumps all at once as a spark when you touch metal.

</details>

2. **Stretch (grades 7–8):** lightning and the spark from a doorknob are the same thing. What is different about them?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Mainly the amount of charge and the distance it jumps. A doorknob spark jumps a few millimetres. Lightning carries vastly more charge across kilometres of air, heating the air so fast it explodes outward: that's thunder.

</details>
