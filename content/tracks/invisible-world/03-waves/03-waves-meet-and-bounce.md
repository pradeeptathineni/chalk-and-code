## Start here

Two pulses race towards each other along a rope, one from each end. When they meet, will they bounce off each other like two balls, or something else?

## The common wrong idea

"Waves crash into each other and bounce, like balls do."

Waves **pass straight through** each other. While they overlap they **add up**, and afterwards each carries on exactly as before, as if the other had never been there.

## The idea

**Reflection.** When a wave hits a wall or the end of a rope, it bounces back. A sound that bounces off a cliff and comes back to you is an **echo**. Light bouncing off a mirror is reflection too.

**Adding up (superposition).** Where two waves overlap, their heights add together:

- **Crest meets crest**: they add up to a bigger crest. Louder sound, taller water wave.
- **Crest meets trough** (up meets down): they **cancel out**, for a moment leaving the rope flat.

**Noise-cancelling headphones** use this trick. A tiny microphone listens to the noise around you, and the speaker plays the exact same sound wave **upside down**. Crest meets trough, and the noise cancels before it reaches your ear.

**Bending (refraction).** When a wave goes from one material into another where it travels at a different speed, it can change direction. That's why a straw in a glass of water looks bent. Light travels more slowly in water than in air, so it changes direction as it leaves the water on its way to your eye.

## Worked example

Two pulses, each 30 cm high, meet in the middle of a rope. How high is the rope where they overlap exactly?

1. Both pulses go up, so their heights add.
2. 0 + 30 = 60$.
3. For an instant the rope rises **60 cm**. Then both pulses carry on at 30 cm each.

## Your turn (faded example)

A pulse 30 cm **up** meets a pulse 30 cm **down**.

1. Heights add: 0 + (-30) = \;?$
2. So at that instant, the rope is ___.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

0: the rope is flat for an instant. Then the two pulses reappear and carry on, as the sim shows.

</details>

## Practice

1. You shout towards a cliff 170 m away and hear the echo 1 second later. Sound travels at about 340 m/s. Does that make sense?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Yes. The sound goes 170 m there and 170 m back, 340 m in total, which takes 340 ÷ 340 = 1 second.

</details>

2. Why do noise-cancelling headphones work best on steady sounds, like a plane engine, and worse on sudden ones, like a voice?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

To cancel a sound, the headphones must play its exact upside-down copy at the same moment. A steady hum is easy to predict and copy. A sudden, changing sound is harder to copy in time.

</details>
