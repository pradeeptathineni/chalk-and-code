## Start here

Point a TV remote at your phone's camera, look at the screen, and press a button. (If nothing shows, try the other camera: some phones filter infrared out.) What do you see, and why can the phone see it when you can't?

## The common wrong idea

"Infrared *is* heat."

Infrared is **light**, just with waves a bit too long for our eyes. It gets mixed up with heat because warm things give off a lot of it, and because things warm up when they soak it in. But a TV remote's infrared beam is far too weak to warm anything; it's just a flashing signal.

## The idea

In 1800 the astronomer William Herschel split sunlight into a rainbow with a prism and put thermometers on each colour. Then he put one just *past* the red end, where there was no colour at all. That one warmed up the most. There was light there that nobody could see: **infrared**, meaning "below red".

Infrared shows up in three ways you'll meet every day:

- **Everything warm glows in infrared.** You, your dog and a cup of cocoa all give it off. Heat cameras and night-vision cameras turn that glow into a picture. Some snakes can sense it, which helps them find warm prey in the dark.
- **Remotes talk in infrared.** A small infrared light on the front of the remote flashes on and off in a pattern, a code for "volume up" or "channel 4". A sensor on the TV reads the flashes. Phone cameras can pick up some infrared, so they show you the flashing.
- **Robots feel their way with infrared.** A robot's obstacle sensor shines an infrared light forward and has a detector next to it. If the light bounces off something and comes back, there's something in front. Nothing comes back? The way is clear.

## Worked example

A robot drives toward a white wall and stops in time. Then it drives toward a black velvet curtain and bumps right into it. Why?

1. The sensor only knows something is there if enough infrared **bounces back**.
2. A white wall reflects most of the light that hits it, infrared included, so plenty comes back.
3. Black velvet soaks up (absorbs) most of the light that hits it, so very little comes back.
4. The detector sees almost nothing and decides the way is clear. **Bump.**

## Your turn (faded example)

The same robot drives toward a big mirror held at an angle, so the mirror faces off to the side.

1. Does the mirror reflect infrared well? ___
2. But which direction does the bounced light go? ___
3. So will the detector, right next to the light, see it? ___

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The mirror reflects infrared very well, but at an angle it sends the light off to the side, away from the detector. The robot sees nothing and may bump into it. Sensors need the light to come back *to them*, not just to bounce.

</details>

## Practice

1. Why can a heat camera find a person hiding in a dark room, but an ordinary camera can't?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The person is warm, so they give off infrared even in the dark. An ordinary camera needs visible light to bounce off them, and there isn't any. The heat camera sees the infrared they give off themselves.

</details>

2. Your remote stops working when you hold your hand over its front end. What does that tell you?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The signal is light, and your hand blocks it, just as it would block a flashlight. Radio signals, which are much longer waves, would get past your hand.

</details>
