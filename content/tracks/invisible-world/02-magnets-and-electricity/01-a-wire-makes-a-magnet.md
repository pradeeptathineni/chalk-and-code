## Start here

Put a compass next to a wire that's connected to a battery. Before you look at the sim: will the compass care? Wires aren't magnets... are they?

## The common wrong idea

"Electricity and magnetism are two separate things that just happen to have similar rules."

For a long time, scientists thought exactly that. Then in 1820, during a lecture, Hans Christian Ørsted noticed a compass needle twitch every time he switched on a current in a nearby wire. **A current makes a magnetic field.** Electricity and magnetism turned out to be two sides of one thing: **electromagnetism**.

## The idea

- Electric **current** is charge on the move, flowing round a circuit.
- Moving charge makes a **magnetic field** around the wire. The field circles the wire, like rings around a finger.
- **Switch the current off**, and the field disappears. The compass swings back to north.
- **Reverse the current**, and the field reverses. The compass swings the other way.
- One straight wire makes a weak field. **Wind the wire into a coil** and the fields from every loop add up. The coil acts just like a bar magnet, with a north pole at one end and a south pole at the other.

## Worked example

A coil has its north pole on the right. A compass sits to the right of the coil. Which way does the compass's north end point?

1. Field lines come **out of** a north pole.
2. To the right of the coil, the field points away from the coil, to the right.
3. A compass's north end points along the field, so it swings **to the right**, away from the coil. (Unlike poles attract: the compass's *south* end is pulled towards the coil's north pole.)

## Your turn (faded example)

You reverse the battery, so the coil's north pole is now on the left.

1. The pole nearest the compass (on the right) is now ___.
2. The compass's north end is attracted to a ___ pole.
3. So the compass swings ___.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The right-hand end is now the south pole. A compass's north end is attracted to a south pole, so the compass now swings to point towards the coil, the other way from before.

</details>

## Practice

1. Why did nobody notice for centuries that wires make magnetic fields?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Until about 1800 there were no batteries, so there was no steady current in wires to notice. And a single straight wire makes only a weak field, so it takes a compass held close by to see it.

</details>

2. How could you make a coil's magnetic field stronger without changing the battery?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Wind more turns of wire, so more loops add their fields together. (The next lesson shows another trick: put iron inside.)

</details>
