> - explain how a motor uses magnets to spin
> - explain how a speaker turns an electric signal into sound
> - explain how a generator makes electricity, and why the magnet has to keep moving

## Start here

A motor turns electricity into movement. Could you run it backwards: turn movement into electricity? Make a guess, then try the **Generator** mode in the sim.

## The common wrong idea

"A magnet sitting inside a coil will make electricity."

It won't. In 1831 Michael Faraday found that a magnet makes current only while it's **moving** (or, more exactly, while the magnetic field through the coil is **changing**). A magnet that just sits there makes nothing at all.

## The idea

**Motors.** Put a coil carrying current between the poles of a magnet. The coil is now an electromagnet, so it's pushed and pulled by the magnet around it, and it turns. Just before it settles, a clever switch reverses the current, so it keeps getting pushed round and round. That's a motor: in fans, drills, toy cars, electric cars and your robot's wheels.

**Speakers.** A speaker has a coil attached to a paper or plastic cone, sitting next to a magnet. The music arrives as an electric current that changes direction hundreds or thousands of times a second. The coil is pushed in and out just as fast, the cone shakes the air, and you hear sound.

**Generators.** Run it backwards: **move a magnet in and out of a coil, and a current flows**. Moving it in pushes current one way, pulling it out pushes it the other way. Move faster, or use more turns, and you get more current. Almost all of the world's electricity comes from this: giant magnets spun inside coils by steam, wind or falling water.

## Worked example

You push a magnet into a coil and the meter swings right. What happens when you pull it back out?

1. Current flows only while the magnet moves.
2. Pulling out is moving the other way, so the change in the field is the other way.
3. The meter swings **left**. Moving it in and out over and over makes a current that keeps switching direction: **alternating current**, the kind in your wall sockets.

## Your turn (faded example)

You hold the magnet still and move the **coil** onto it instead.

1. Is the magnetic field through the coil changing? ___
2. So does current flow? ___

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Yes and yes. It doesn't matter which one moves: what matters is that the field through the coil changes. That's why some generators spin the magnet and others spin the coil.

</details>

## Practice

1. A wind turbine stops on a still day. Why does it stop making electricity, even though its magnets are still inside its coils?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No wind means no spinning, so the field through the coils stops changing, and no current is made. A generator needs motion.

</details>

2. **Stretch (grades 7–8):** a bike light is powered by a little generator rubbing on the wheel. Why does the light get brighter as you pedal faster?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Faster pedalling spins the generator's magnet faster, so the field through its coil changes faster, which pushes more current through the bulb.

</details>
