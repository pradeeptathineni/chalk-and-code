## Start here

A bat flies through a pitch-black cave, swerves around rocks and snatches a moth the size of your fingernail out of the air. It isn't using its eyes. What is it using?

## The common wrong idea

"Bats are blind, so they just fly around and hope."

Bats can see, but at night they rely on something better: **echolocation**. They shout, then listen for the echo. Their shouts are ultrasound, far too high for us to hear, and some are louder than a smoke alarm. Good thing we can't hear them!

## The idea

1. The bat sends out a short, very high-pitched call.
2. The sound bounces off anything in front of it: a wall, a branch, a moth.
3. The bat hears the echo. The **longer the echo takes, the further away** the object is. Which ear hears it first tells it which side the object is on.

Why such high pitches? A wave can only bounce off things that are about its own size or bigger. A bat call at 50,000 Hz has waves about **7 mm** long, so small that they bounce off a tiny insect. A low sound would wash right past it.

Dolphins do the same underwater, clicking up to 150,000 times a second. Ships use it too and call it **sonar**. And doctors use ultrasound at millions of vibrations a second to make pictures of the inside of the body, like a baby before it's born. Sound bounces differently off bone, muscle and fluid, so the echoes draw a picture, without any of the harm X-rays could do.

## Worked example

A bat calls and hears the echo from a wall 0.1 seconds later. Sound travels at 340 m/s. How far away is the wall?

1. In 0.1 s, sound travels 40 \times 0.1 = 34$ m.
2. But that's there **and back**.
3. So the wall is 4 \div 2 = $ **17 m** away.

## Your turn (faded example)

The echo from a moth comes back after 0.01 seconds.

1. Distance there and back $= 340 \times 0.01 = \;?$
2. Distance to the moth $= \;? \div 2$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

40 \times 0.01 = 3.4$ m there and back, so the moth is **1.7 m** away. The bat has a fraction of a second to catch it, and it does this over and over, sending more and more calls per second as it closes in.

</details>

## Practice

1. Why do you have to halve the distance in an echo problem?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The sound travels to the object and back again, so the total path is twice the distance to the object.

</details>

2. Sound travels about 1,500 m/s in water, much faster than in air. A dolphin's click echoes off a fish after 0.02 seconds. How far is the fish?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{,}500 \times 0.02 = 30$ m there and back, so the fish is **15 m** away.

</details>
