## Start here

When a neighbour plays music loudly, what do you hear through the wall: the singing, the guitar, or the thumping bass? Why that part?

## The common wrong idea

"High sounds and low sounds travel the same way, just at different pitches."

All sound travels at the same speed in air, but **low sounds travel much further** before fading away. Walls, air and trees soak up high sounds quickly. Long, low waves roll on through. That's why the bass makes it through the wall, and why thunder far away is a low rumble while thunder close by is a sharp crack.

## The idea

**Infrasound** is sound below about 20 Hz, too low for human ears. Its waves are enormous: a 10 Hz wave in air is 34 metres long, longer than a basketball court.

- **Elephants** rumble to each other at pitches near the bottom edge of our hearing and below. The low rumbles carry for **kilometres**, so herds far apart can keep in touch. Elephants may even feel some of the vibrations through the ground with their feet.
- **Blue whales**, the biggest animals ever, sing at very low pitches. Low sound travels astonishingly well in water, so their songs can cross **hundreds of kilometres** of ocean.
- **The Earth rumbles.** Earthquakes, volcanoes and even big ocean storms make infrasound. Scientists keep a worldwide network of infrasound listening stations, and they can hear a volcano erupt from the other side of the planet.
- Some people feel infrasound as an odd unease or pressure, even though they can't hear it.

## Worked example

How long is an elephant's 15 Hz rumble in air?

1. wavelength = speed ÷ frequency.
2. 40 \div 15 \approx 23$.
3. Each wave is about **23 metres** long, as long as two buses.

## Your turn (faded example)

A blue whale sings at 17 Hz underwater, where sound travels at about 1,500 m/s.

1. wavelength = 1,500 ÷ ___
2. wavelength ≈ ___ m

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{,}500 \div 17 \approx 88$ m. Each wave of the whale's song is nearly as long as a football field.

</details>

## Practice

1. Thunder close by sounds like a sharp crack. Thunder far away sounds like a low rumble. Why?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The air soaks up the high-pitched parts of the sound faster than the low parts. By the time distant thunder reaches you, mostly the low rumble is left.

</details>

2. Why would elephants use very low sounds to talk across long distances, and not high ones?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Low sounds lose less energy as they travel and pass around trees and hills more easily, so they carry much further across the savanna.

</details>
