> - explain what sound is, in terms of air being squeezed and stretched

## Start here

Some shops once played a very high-pitched buzz outside their doors to keep teenagers from hanging around. Adults walking past heard nothing at all. How can a sound be loud for one person and silent for another?

## The common wrong idea

"If a sound is there, everyone hears it the same way."

Your ears only pick up a certain range of pitches, and that range **shrinks as you get older**, from the top. Most children can hear up to about 20,000 vibrations a second. Many adults can't hear anything above 12,000 or so. The sound is there either way; some ears just can't catch it.

## The idea

A sound starts with something **vibrating**: a guitar string, a speaker cone, your vocal cords. Each vibration squeezes the air next to it, then lets it stretch out again. Those squeezes pass from air to air as a **longitudinal wave**, at about **340 metres every second**, until they reach your eardrum and make it vibrate too.

- **Pitch is frequency.** Fast vibrations (many per second) sound high. Slow vibrations sound low.
- **Loudness is amplitude.** Bigger squeezes carry more energy and sound louder.

People hear from about **20 Hz** (20 vibrations a second, a deep rumble) to about **20,000 Hz** (a thin, piercing whine). Below that is **infrasound**. Above that is **ultrasound**. Plenty of animals live in those ranges, holding whole conversations you will never hear.

## Worked example

A guitar string vibrates 110 times a second. How long is each sound wave in air?

1. wavelength = speed ÷ frequency.
2. 40 \div 110 \approx 3.1$.
3. Each wave is about **3 metres** long, longer than a car is wide.

## Your turn (faded example)

A whistle vibrates 3,400 times a second.

1. wavelength = 340 ÷ ___
2. wavelength = ___ m = ___ cm

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

40 \div 3{,}400 = 0.1$ m, which is 10 cm. Higher pitch, shorter wave.

</details>

## Practice

1. You pluck a guitar string harder. Does the pitch change, the loudness, or both?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The loudness. Plucking harder makes bigger vibrations (more amplitude), but the string still vibrates the same number of times a second, so the pitch stays the same.

</details>

2. Why can't sound travel through space?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sound is squeezed and stretched air (or water, or rock). Space is almost empty, with nothing to squeeze, so there's nothing to carry the wave. Light doesn't need anything to travel through, which is why we can see the Sun but never hear it.

</details>
