## Start here

You are slightly radioactive. So is your lunch, especially if it has a banana in it. So are the bricks in your house and the air you breathe. Is that something to worry about?

## The common wrong idea

"Any radiation at all is dangerous, and only nuclear accidents have it."

Radiation has been all around us since before life began. It comes from rocks, from space, and from inside our own bodies. This everyday **background radiation** is small, and life has always lived with it. What matters is **how much**, and for how long.

## The idea

**Where natural radiation comes from:**
- **Rocks and soil**, which contain small amounts of uranium and other radioactive atoms. Some give off radon gas, which can collect in basements.
- **Space**: fast particles called cosmic rays rain down all the time. On a long plane flight, higher up with less air above you to block them, you get a little more.
- **Food and your body**: bananas contain potassium, and a tiny part of all potassium is radioactive. So does your body.

**Radioactivity put to work:**
- **Smoke alarms**: many have a speck of a radioactive element called americium inside. Its alpha radiation lets a tiny current flow through the air; smoke blocks it and sets off the alarm.
- **Medicine**: doctors aim gamma rays at tumours to destroy cancer cells, and inject short-half-life "tracers" that show how organs are working.
- **Power**: nuclear power stations use the heat from splitting uranium atoms to boil water and spin generators, the same way as in [Motors, speakers and generators](../02-magnets-and-electricity/03-motors-and-generators.md).
- **Dating**: carbon-14 tells us the age of ancient bones and wood. Uranium tells us the age of the Earth's oldest rocks.

**The three rules for staying safe**, used by everyone who works with radiation:
1. **Time**: spend as little time near it as you can.
2. **Distance**: stay as far away as you can. Radiation spreads out, so moving twice as far away cuts it to a quarter.
3. **Shielding**: put something in the way: paper for alpha, metal for beta, lead or concrete for gamma.

## Worked example

A doctor uses a tracer with a half-life of 6 hours. What fraction is left in the body after one day?

1. One day is 24 hours, which is 4 \div 6 = 4$ half-lives.
2. Halve four times:  \to \frac12 \to \frac14 \to \frac18 \to \frac{1}{16}$.
3. Only **1/16** is left after a day (and the body flushes some out too). That's why short half-lives are chosen for tracers.

## Your turn (faded example)

You stand 1 metre from a weak source, then step back to 3 metres.

1. You're ___ times further away.
2. Radiation spreads out, so it drops by  \times 3 = \;?$
3. You get about ___ of what you got before.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

3 times further away, so the radiation drops by  \times 3 = 9$. You get about one-ninth as much.

</details>

## Practice

1. Why do dentists step out of the room, or behind a screen, when they take an X-ray?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Distance and shielding. They take many X-rays every day, so even small amounts would add up. Stepping away and behind a screen keeps their dose tiny.

</details>

2. Why do people sometimes test their basements for radon?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Radon is a radioactive gas from rocks and soil that can collect in low, closed spaces. Breathing it in over many years brings alpha radiation inside the lungs, where nothing shields the cells, so homes with lots of it are fixed with better ventilation.

</details>
