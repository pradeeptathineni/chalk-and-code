## Start here

In 1896, the scientist Henri Becquerel wrapped some photographic plates in thick black paper, put them in a drawer next to a lump of uranium, and forgot about them. When he developed them, they were fogged, as if light had got in. But no light could have. What got through?

## The common wrong idea

"Atoms last forever and never change."

Most atoms do stay the same forever. But some atoms are **unstable**. Sooner or later they fall apart into a different kind of atom, firing out a tiny burst of energy or particles as they go. That's **radioactivity**, and it's what fogged Becquerel's plates through the paper.

## The idea

**Inside an atom.** Every atom has a tiny, heavy centre called the **nucleus**, made of **protons** and **neutrons**, with **electrons** whizzing around far outside it. The nucleus is astonishingly small: if an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be a pea on the centre spot.

**Unstable nuclei.** In some nuclei, the protons and neutrons aren't in a comfortable balance. At some random moment, the nucleus changes to become more stable, and throws out **radiation**:

| Radiation | What it is | Stopped by |
|---|---|---|
| **Alpha** | a small chunk of nucleus (2 protons, 2 neutrons) | a sheet of paper, or your skin |
| **Beta** | a fast electron | a thin sheet of aluminum |
| **Gamma** | very high-energy light (see [Ultraviolet and beyond](../04-light-we-cant-see/04-ultraviolet-and-beyond.md)) | thick lead or concrete, and even then not all of it |

**Marie Curie** took up Becquerel's mystery, named it "radioactivity", and discovered two brand-new elements, polonium and radium. She became the first person to win two Nobel prizes, in two different sciences. Nobody then knew how dangerous radiation could be, and her notebooks are **still radioactive** today, over a century later. Visitors to the library that keeps them must sign a form and wear protective clothing.

## Worked example

A lump of radioactive material is sealed in a thick paper envelope. Which kinds of radiation could get out?

1. Alpha is stopped by paper, so **no alpha**.
2. Beta needs metal to stop it, so **beta gets out**.
3. Gamma needs thick lead or concrete, so **gamma gets out**.

## Your turn (faded example)

The same lump is put in a box of thin aluminum.

1. Alpha: stopped by ___, so ___.
2. Beta: stopped by ___, so ___.
3. Gamma: ___.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Alpha is stopped (paper would do it, so aluminum certainly does). Beta is stopped by the aluminum. Only gamma gets out.

</details>

## Practice

1. What fogged Becquerel's plates through the black paper?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Radiation from the uranium. The paper blocked light, but some of the radiation from the unstable uranium nuclei went right through it.

</details>

2. Why is alpha radiation fairly harmless outside your body, but dangerous if you breathe it in?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Outside, your dead outer layer of skin stops it. Breathed in, there's nothing to stop it reaching living cells in your lungs, where it deposits all its energy in a tiny spot.

</details>
