> - explain what air pressure is, using the idea of particles
> - say roughly how hard the air pushes on you
> - explain why you don't feel squashed

## Start here

Hold your hand flat, palm up. The air is pressing down on it with about the weight of a large adult. Why can't you feel it?

## The common wrong idea

"Air is nothing. Empty space with a bit of smell in it."

Air is **stuff**. It's made of tiny particles, mostly nitrogen and oxygen, far too small to see, zooming around at about **470 metres per second**, faster than a jet airliner. Each one crashes into everything in its way, trillions of trillions of times a second on every patch of your skin. Each crash is a tiny push. All together they add up to **air pressure**.

## The idea

We live at the bottom of an ocean of air about 100 km deep. All that air above us has weight, and it squashes the air down here, packing the particles close together so they push hard.

How hard? About **1 kilogram's weight on every square centimetre**, roughly the size of your fingernail. On a car roof, that's about 10 tonnes, the weight of an elephant or two.

So why don't we get crushed? Because air pushes **in every direction**: down, up, sideways, and from inside us too. The air in your lungs and the fluids in your body push back just as hard. Everything balances, so you feel nothing at all.

You only notice air pressure when the push on one side is **different** from the push on the other. In 1654, Otto von Guericke put two metal half-spheres together and pumped the air out from inside. With no air pushing outwards, the air outside held them together so hard that two teams of horses couldn't pull them apart.

## Worked example

A tabletop is 100 cm by 50 cm. Roughly how much weight is the air pressing down on it with?

1. Area $= 100 \times 50 = 5{,}000$ square centimetres.
2. About 1 kg on each square centimetre, so {,}000$ kg.
3. That's **5 tonnes**, about the weight of an elephant. The table doesn't collapse because air underneath pushes up just as hard.

## Your turn (faded example)

A sheet of paper is about 20 cm by 30 cm.

1. Area $= 20 \times 30 = \;?$ square centimetres.
2. Air pushes on it with about ___ kg.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

600 square centimetres, so about 600 kg, the weight of a small car, pressing on a sheet of paper. And the same push from below, which is why it doesn't move.

</details>

## Practice

1. If you pump all the air out of a plastic bottle, it crumples. Why?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Inside, there's no air left to push outwards. Outside, the air still pushes inwards with its full weight, and the plastic isn't strong enough to resist.

</details>

2. Why couldn't the horses pull von Guericke's spheres apart, but once he let air back in they fell apart easily?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

With no air inside, nothing pushed the halves apart, while the outside air pushed them together with tonnes of force. Letting air in made the push inside match the push outside, so nothing was holding them together.

</details>
