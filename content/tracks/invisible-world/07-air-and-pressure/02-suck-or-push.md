## Start here

When you drink through a straw, do you pull the drink up? Here's a puzzle: no matter how strong your lungs are, you could never drink water through a straw more than about **10 metres** tall. Why would there be a limit?

## The common wrong idea

"Suction pulls things."

There's no such thing as a pull from "suction". When you sip on a straw, you **lower the air pressure** inside your mouth. The air outside is still pushing down on the drink in the glass with its full weight, and that **push** forces the drink up the straw into the space where the pressure is lower.

## The idea

- **Squeeze air into a smaller space** and the same particles hit the walls more often: **higher pressure**.
- **Give air more space** and they hit less often: **lower pressure**.
- Stuff gets pushed from **high pressure towards low pressure**.

**Straws.** Your cheeks and tongue make the space in your mouth bigger, lowering the pressure there. The air pressing on the drink in the glass pushes it up the straw. And the 10-metre limit? Even if you took *all* the air out of the straw, the outside air can only push water up about 10 metres before the water's own weight balances the push.

**Suction cups.** Pressing a cup flat squeezes most of the air out from under it. When the rubber springs back a little, the space under it gets bigger and the pressure drops. Now the outside air pushes the cup against the wall much harder than the little air left inside pushes it away.

**Syringes and your lungs.** Pull the plunger and the space inside gets bigger, the pressure drops, and outside air (or medicine) is pushed in. Your lungs work the same way: a muscle under them called the diaphragm pulls down, your chest gets bigger, the pressure inside drops, and the outside air pushes in. That's every breath you take.

## Worked example

The sim shows normal pressure in a box of size 500. You push the piston to 250. What happens to the pressure?

1. Same particles, same speed, but half the space.
2. Each particle crosses the box and hits a wall twice as often.
3. So the pressure roughly **doubles**: about 2 × normal.

## Your turn (faded example)

Now you pull the piston out from 250 to 500.

1. The space has ___ (doubled / halved).
2. So the particles hit the walls ___ as often.
3. The pressure ___.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The space doubled, so the particles hit the walls half as often, and the pressure halves, back to normal.

</details>

## Practice

1. Why is it hard to drink through a straw with a tiny hole in its side?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Air rushes in through the hole, so you can't lower the pressure inside the straw. Without the difference in pressure, the outside air has nothing to push the drink towards.

</details>

2. Why does a suction cup fall off a rough wall but stick to smooth glass?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

On a rough wall, air leaks in through the gaps under the rubber, so the pressure underneath rises back to normal and nothing holds the cup on. Glass seals tightly, keeping the pressure under the cup low.

</details>
