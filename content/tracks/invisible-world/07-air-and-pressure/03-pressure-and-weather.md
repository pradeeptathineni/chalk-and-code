> - explain why air pressure drops as you go higher

> - connect high and low pressure to the weather

## Start here

Take a sealed bag of chips up a mountain or on a plane, and it puffs up like a pillow. Your ears pop on the way too. Nobody opened the bag. What changed?

## The common wrong idea

"Air pressure is the same everywhere."

Air pressure changes with **height**, with **temperature**, and from **day to day**. Those changes are why your ears pop, why car tyres look flatter on cold mornings, and why it rains.

## The idea

**Higher up, less air on top.** At the top of a tall mountain, there's less air above you squashing the air down, so the pressure is lower. At the top of Mount Everest it's only about a third of the pressure at sea level. The chip bag was sealed at the factory near sea level, so the air inside pushes outward harder than the thin mountain air pushes in, and the bag swells. Your ears pop when the air trapped behind your eardrums escapes to match the new pressure outside.

**Hotter air pushes harder.** Heat air and its particles move faster. They hit the walls more often and harder, so in a closed container the pressure goes **up**. That's why a ball left in a hot car gets rock hard, and why tyres look a little flat on a cold morning.

**Weather.** The Sun heats the Earth unevenly, so some places end up with slightly higher air pressure and some with lower. Air is pushed from high pressure towards low, and that moving air is **wind**. Where the pressure is **low**, air rises, cools and forms clouds: often rain. Where it's **high**, air sinks and the sky clears. A **barometer** measures air pressure, and a falling barometer is an old sign that a storm is coming.

## Worked example

A sealed bottle of air at room temperature (20 °C) is heated in hot water to about 90 °C. Does the pressure inside go up, down, or stay the same?

1. The bottle is sealed, so it's the same amount of air in the same space.
2. Heating makes the particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and harder.
3. The pressure goes **up**. (If the cap is loose, it may pop off!)

## Your turn (faded example)

A balloon blown up indoors is taken outside on a freezing day.

1. The air particles inside move ___ (faster / slower).
2. They push on the inside of the balloon ___ (more / less).
3. The balloon ___.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Slower, less, and the balloon shrinks a little. Bring it back inside and it grows again.

</details>

## Practice

1. Why might a plastic water bottle you emptied on a plane be crushed by the time you land?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

If you closed it up high, it's full of the lower-pressure cabin air. On the ground the air pressure is higher, so the outside air pushes in harder than the air inside pushes out, and the bottle crumples.

</details>

2. The weather forecast shows a big area of low pressure heading your way. What kind of weather might you expect?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Clouds and probably rain, maybe wind. In low pressure, air rises and cools, and its water vapour turns into cloud.

</details>
