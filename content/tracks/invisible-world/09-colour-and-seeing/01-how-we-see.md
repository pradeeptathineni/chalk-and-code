## Start here

In a room with no light at all, can your eyes get used to the dark and eventually see? Most people think yes. Think again before you read on: what do eyes actually need?

## The common wrong idea

"We see by sending something out from our eyes."

People believed that for thousands of years. It's the other way round: light comes **into** your eyes. You see a book because light from a lamp or the Sun **bounces off** the book and some of it lands in your eye. In a room with truly no light, nothing bounces, and you see nothing at all, however long you wait.

## The idea

Light enters your eye through the dark hole in the middle, the **pupil**. A lens focuses it onto the back of the eye, the **retina**, which is covered in millions of tiny light sensors.

- **Rods** work in dim light. They can't tell colours apart, which is why everything looks grey by moonlight.
- **Cones** work in brighter light and see colour. There are only **three kinds**: one most sensitive to red light, one to green, one to blue.

So how do you see yellow, pink, brown and millions of other colours with only three sensors? Your brain compares how strongly each kind of cone is responding. Yellow light makes your red and green cones respond about equally, and your brain says "yellow". That's also why **red and green light mixed together look yellow** to you, even though no yellow light is there at all.

Other animals see differently. Dogs have only two kinds of cone, so reds and greens look alike to them. Many birds and insects have a fourth kind that sees ultraviolet, a colour we can't even imagine.

## Worked example

A banana looks yellow. What light is actually reaching your eye from it?

1. White light from the Sun contains all colours.
2. The banana reflects mostly red and green light, and soaks up the blue.
3. Red and green light reach your eye, your red and green cones both respond, and your brain calls it **yellow**.

## Your turn (faded example)

A blueberry looks blue.

1. The blueberry reflects mostly ___ light.
2. That light excites your ___ cones.
3. Your brain calls it ___.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It reflects mostly blue light, which excites your blue cones, and your brain calls it blue.

</details>

## Practice

1. Why do colours fade at dusk, even though you can still see shapes?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

As light fades, your cones stop working well and your rods take over. Rods can't tell colours apart, so the world looks grey.

</details>

2. Why can't you see in a completely dark room, even after an hour?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Seeing needs light to bounce off things into your eyes. With no light at all, there's nothing to bounce, so nothing reaches your eyes.

</details>
