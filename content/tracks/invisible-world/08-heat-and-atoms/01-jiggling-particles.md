> **After this lesson you can:**
> - explain that everything is made of tiny particles called atoms and molecules
> - describe how small and how many they are
> - give evidence that the particles are always moving

## Start here

Drop a little food colouring into a glass of still water and don't stir. Come back in ten minutes. The colour has spread all through the glass. Nobody touched it. What moved it?

## The common wrong idea

"Solid things are solid all the way through, and things that look still are still."

Everything, your desk, the air, your hand, is made of **particles** far too small to see: **atoms**, often joined into groups called **molecules**. And they are **never still**. In a solid they jiggle on the spot. In a liquid they slide past each other. In a gas they fly about freely. The food colouring spreads because the water molecules keep bumping it around.

## The idea

**How small?** Line up water molecules side by side and you'd need about **three and a half million** of them to make a row one millimetre long, a little more than the thickness of a credit card.

**How many?** There are more water molecules in one glass of water than there are glasses of water in all the oceans on Earth.

**How do we know they're moving?** In 1827 a scientist called Robert Brown looked through a microscope at tiny specks of pollen in water. They were dancing about in little zigzags, never stopping. Nobody could explain it until 1905, when Albert Einstein worked out that the specks were being bombarded by water molecules too small to see, unevenly, from all sides. That was some of the strongest evidence that atoms are real.

The particles also **pull on each other** a little when they're close. How strongly they pull, and how hard they jiggle, decides whether something is a solid, a liquid or a gas.

## Worked example

Why does food colouring spread faster in hot water than in cold water?

1. In hot water, the water molecules jiggle and move faster.
2. They bump the colouring molecules harder and more often.
3. So the colouring spreads **faster**. (Try it with two glasses side by side!)

## Your turn (faded example)

You open a bottle of perfume at one end of a room, and a minute later someone at the other end smells it.

1. Perfume is made of molecules, and they ___ through the air.
2. Air molecules keep ___ into them, spreading them out.
3. So the smell ___.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The perfume molecules fly off into the air, get bumped around by the air molecules, and spread out until some reach the other person's nose.

</details>

## Practice

1. Can you see a single atom with an ordinary microscope?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No. Atoms are thousands of times smaller than the smallest thing light microscopes can show. Scientists use special microscopes that "feel" the surface with an incredibly sharp tip to make pictures of atoms.

</details>

2. Why don't you notice the particles in your desk moving?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

They only jiggle on the spot, over tiny distances, and they all jiggle in different directions, so on average the desk stays put.

</details>
