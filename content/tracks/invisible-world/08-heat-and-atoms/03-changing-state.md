> **After this lesson you can:**
> - describe solids, liquids and gases in terms of particles
> - explain melting and boiling as a contest between jiggling and sticking
> - explain why sweating cools you down, and why ice floats

## Start here

Ice is frozen water. So why does ice float on water instead of sinking? Almost every other solid sinks in its own liquid. Is water just weird?

## The common wrong idea

"When ice melts or water boils, it turns into a different substance."

It's the same molecules the whole time. Ice, water and steam are all made of exactly the same water molecules. The only thing that changes is how fast they're jiggling, and so how tightly they hold on to each other.

## The idea

Particles **jiggle** (more when hotter) and **stick** to their neighbours (a little). The state depends on which wins:

| State | Particles | Shape and size |
|---|---|---|
| **Solid** | locked in a pattern, jiggling on the spot | keeps its shape |
| **Liquid** | touching but sliding past each other | flows, takes the shape of its container |
| **Gas** | far apart, flying freely | spreads out to fill any space |

**Melting:** heat a solid, the jiggling gets strong enough to break the particles out of their pattern. **Boiling:** heat a liquid enough and particles break free from each other entirely and fly off as a gas.

**Why sweating cools you.** Even below boiling, the very fastest particles at the surface of a liquid can escape: that's **evaporation**. Since the fastest (hottest) ones leave, the ones left behind are slower on average, so the liquid gets cooler. When sweat evaporates off your skin, it carries heat away with it.

**Why ice floats.** Water is unusual. As it freezes, its molecules lock into an open, six-sided pattern with lots of empty space (the same six sides you see in every snowflake), so ice takes up about **9% more room** than the water it came from. Bigger but the same weight means lighter for its size, so it floats. That's why lakes freeze from the top down, keeping fish alive underneath, and why a full water bottle left in the freezer can burst.

## Worked example

You leave a puddle out on a warm day. It's not boiling, but by evening it's gone. Where did it go?

1. The water molecules are all jiggling, some faster than others.
2. The fastest ones at the surface break free and fly off into the air as water vapour.
3. Bit by bit, the whole puddle **evaporates** into the air, without ever boiling.

## Your turn (faded example)

You step out of a swimming pool on a windy day and feel freezing.

1. Water on your skin starts to ___.
2. The fastest molecules leave, carrying ___ away from your skin.
3. Wind blows the escaped molecules away, so even ___ can escape.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The water evaporates, the fastest molecules carry heat away from your skin, and the wind sweeps the vapour away so evaporation keeps going quickly. That's why you feel colder in the wind than standing still.

</details>

## Practice

1. Why does a puddle dry faster on a hot, windy day than on a cool, still day?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Heat makes more molecules fast enough to escape, and wind carries escaped molecules away so they don't fall back in.

</details>

2. Why do water pipes sometimes burst in very cold weather?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

When the water inside freezes, it expands by about 9%. The pipe can't stretch that much, so it cracks.

</details>
